What Is an Affidavit of Heirship? How to Transfer Property Without Probate

Probate can take 1–2 years and cost 3–8% of the estate's value. In states that allow it, an affidavit of heirship is a faster, cheaper alternative for real property transfers.

What an Affidavit of Heirship Does

An affidavit of heirship is a sworn statement by two or more disinterested witnesses (people who are not heirs and have nothing to gain from the estate) that describes: the decedent's identity, date of death, marital history, children and other heirs, whether the decedent left a will, and any debts secured by the property. Once recorded in the county deed records and held for a statutory period (typically 5 years in Texas), it can be used to establish that heirs have clear title.

It does not require a court proceeding — just two witnesses, a notary, and the county recorder's filing fee.

Which States Allow It

Affidavit of heirship procedures are most well-established in: Texas (5-year holding period, then usable to convey title), Colorado, Kansas, Nebraska, Oklahoma, and several other states. California allows a "Petition to Determine Succession to Real Property" for smaller estates. Some states use similar instruments under different names.

California, New York, Florida, and other states with robust probate alternatives (small estate affidavits, summary administration, transfer-on-death deeds) may not recognize affidavits of heirship for real property. Check your state's probate code or consult a local estate attorney.

Requirements for a Valid Affidavit of Heirship

In Texas (the most widely used version): (1) Signed by two disinterested witnesses who personally knew the decedent. (2) Witnesses must state they are not heirs and have no interest in the estate. (3) Notarized. (4) Recorded in the county real property records where the property is located. (5) Held of record for 5 years before it establishes clear title.

The affidavit must accurately describe the property (legal description from the deed, not just the address), the decedent's full identity and date of death, and all heirs — even those who may not be taking the property.

Limitations and Risks

An affidavit of heirship does not: clear title to property encumbered by a mortgage (the lender may still have rights), address community property claims from a surviving spouse, resolve disputes among competing heirs, or override a valid will (if the decedent left a will, probate is required). Title insurance companies may be reluctant to insure property transferred by affidavit of heirship without the 5-year holding period having elapsed.

For estates with significant assets, multiple properties, debts, or family disputes, a formal probate proceeding provides stronger legal protection for all parties. The affidavit of heirship is best suited for simple situations: one property, no mortgage, no disputes among heirs.

FAQ

Can an heir sign their own affidavit of heirship?
No. The witnesses must be "disinterested" — they cannot be heirs, legatees, or anyone who would benefit from the estate. Typically, long-time family friends, neighbors, clergy, or coworkers who knew the decedent well serve as witnesses. If an heir signs, the affidavit is invalid.
What if there is a will?
If the decedent left a will, the estate generally must go through probate (or a simplified probate process for small estates). An affidavit of heirship is typically used when there is no will, not when there is one. Using an affidavit to bypass a valid will could be challenged by other heirs or legatees.
Does an affidavit of heirship transfer title immediately?
In most states, recording the affidavit gives heirs color of title but the chain of title is not considered fully established until the holding period expires (5 years in Texas). During that period, the affidavit can be challenged. After the holding period, the title becomes much more difficult to challenge and title insurance companies will generally insure it.
Can I use an affidavit of heirship to sell the property?
After the holding period (5 years in Texas), generally yes — you can convey the property using a deed that references the recorded affidavit as the basis for title. Before the holding period, some buyers and title companies may require additional documentation or a quiet title lawsuit to insure the transaction.
