Can a Landlord Enter Without Notice? State-by-State Rules

Unauthorized landlord entry is one of the most common tenant complaints — and one of the most misunderstood areas of landlord-tenant law.

The General Rule: 24–48 Hours Notice Required

The majority of US states require landlords to give 24 hours advance written notice before entering a rental unit for non-emergency purposes (inspections, repairs, showings). States requiring 24-hour notice include: California, Florida, New York, Texas (no specific requirement, but reasonable notice applies), Illinois, and most others.

States requiring 48 hours notice: New Hampshire, Vermont, Wisconsin, Maine. Most states allow entry only during reasonable hours — typically business hours, Monday through Friday 8AM–6PM, unless the tenant agrees otherwise.

What Counts as an Emergency (No Notice Required)

All states allow landlords to enter without notice in genuine emergencies: fire, flood, burst pipes, gas leak, suspected break-in, or any situation that poses an immediate threat to the property or the safety of occupants.

Important: the landlord must genuinely believe an emergency exists. Using "emergency" as a pretext to enter without notice for a non-emergency purpose is an illegal entry. If a landlord enters claiming an emergency but there clearly wasn't one, it can be treated as a harassment or illegal entry.

Permitted Purposes for Entry

Even with proper notice, landlords can only enter for specific purposes: making repairs or inspections, showing the unit to prospective tenants or buyers, evaluating the unit after the tenant has given notice to vacate, responding to a court order, or in emergencies.

Landlords cannot use their key to enter simply because they own the property. Tenants have a right to "quiet enjoyment" — peaceful use of the rental without undue interference from the landlord.

Tenant Rights When a Landlord Enters Illegally

If a landlord enters without proper notice or for unauthorized purposes, tenants have remedies: (1) Written demand — send a letter documenting the unauthorized entry and demanding it stop. (2) Complaint to housing authority — many cities have tenant affairs offices that investigate unauthorized entry complaints. (3) Lawsuit for damages — California, for example, allows tenants to sue for actual damages or $100 per violation, plus attorney's fees. (4) Lease termination — repeated unauthorized entries may constitute a breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment, allowing the tenant to terminate and sue for constructive eviction.

FAQ

Does a landlord need notice to enter for repairs they scheduled?
Yes. Even if the landlord previously told you a repair person would come "sometime next week," a specific written notice (typically 24 hours before) is still required in most states. General advance notice of upcoming maintenance doesn't satisfy the entry notice requirement.
Can a tenant refuse a landlord's entry?
A tenant can refuse entry if the landlord did not provide legally required notice, if the landlord is entering at an unreasonable hour, or if the purpose of entry is not legally permitted. Tenants should be cautious about refusing entry for legitimate repair needs — repeated refusals can be a lease violation.
Can a landlord show the unit to buyers without notice?
No. Showing the unit to prospective buyers or tenants requires the same advance notice as any other entry — 24–48 hours in most states. During the final months of a fixed-term lease, some states reduce this to 24 hours and limit showings to reasonable hours.
What if the lease says the landlord can enter anytime?
Such a clause is void in most states. Tenant right-to-notice protection is created by statute and cannot be waived by lease agreement. A landlord who enters in violation of the statutory requirement has committed an illegal entry regardless of what the lease says.
