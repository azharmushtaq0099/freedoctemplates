Evicting a Tenant Without a Written Lease: What Landlords Need to Know

A written lease is not required for a landlord-tenant relationship to exist — and the eviction process still applies to tenants without one. But there are important differences.

Verbal Leases and Month-to-Month Tenancies

A verbal lease agreement is legally binding in most states for tenancies of one year or less. If a tenant moves in, pays rent, and the landlord accepts it, a tenancy exists even without a written lease. The terms are typically inferred from the payment pattern (monthly rent payment = monthly tenancy) and local law.

To evict a tenant without a written lease (or with an expired lease who has become a holdover tenant), the landlord must first terminate the tenancy with proper notice, then file for eviction if the tenant doesn't leave. The process is the same as for a written lease — just without the written documentation.

Required Notice Without a Lease

For month-to-month tenancies (the most common "no lease" situation), notice periods to terminate are set by state law: 30 days in most states, 60 days for tenants in California who have lived there over 1 year, 1 full rental period in Texas and Florida. Some states require the notice to terminate at the end of a rental period — not just 30 days from today.

If the termination is for cause (non-payment, property damage), the standard pay-or-quit or cure-or-quit notice periods apply regardless of whether there is a written lease.

Holdover Tenants

A holdover tenant is one whose lease expired and has not signed a renewal, but continues to occupy the unit and pay rent. If the landlord accepts rent after the lease expires, most states automatically convert the tenancy to month-to-month on the same terms as the expired lease. The landlord then follows month-to-month termination rules.

If the landlord does not want to renew and the tenant stays past the lease expiration, the landlord can either treat the tenant as a trespasser (evict immediately in some states) or as a holdover month-to-month tenant (serve the standard termination notice). Do not accept rent after the lease expires if you want to treat the tenant as a trespasser — accepting payment creates a new tenancy.

Documenting the Tenancy Without a Lease

When evicting without a written lease, documentation is critical: bank statements showing rent payments (establishing the tenancy and the rent amount), any text messages or emails about the tenancy, witnesses who can confirm move-in date. The court needs to establish that a landlord-tenant relationship existed, what the rent was, and when the notice was served.

If you're currently accepting cash for rent with no documentation, start issuing written receipts immediately — both to create a paper trail and because several states require landlords to provide receipts for cash rent payments.

FAQ

Can a landlord evict a family member without a lease?
Yes, but it's complicated. If a family member lives with you and pays rent (even informally), a landlord-tenant relationship likely exists — and you must follow the eviction process to remove them. If they never paid rent and you want them to leave, the eviction process may still apply depending on your state's definition of a tenancy. In some states, this is a "licensor-licensee" relationship with shorter notice periods.
How do you prove a tenancy existed if there's no written lease?
Courts look at: bank records showing rent payments, checks or Venmo/CashApp receipts naming the parties and the property, text or email conversations about the tenancy, utility bills in the tenant's name at the address, mail in the tenant's name, and any witnesses. Even without a written lease, a consistent payment history is strong evidence of a tenancy.
Can a landlord charge more rent without a written lease?
A landlord can propose a rent increase on a month-to-month tenancy (written or verbal) with proper notice — 30 days in most states, 90 days in California for increases over 10%. The tenant can accept the increase (by paying the new amount) or vacate. Without a written agreement specifying the rent, the last regularly paid amount is typically what the court recognizes.
Is a text message lease agreement legally binding?
Potentially yes. Courts in many states have held that text message exchanges can form a binding contract if they meet the basic contract requirements: offer, acceptance, and consideration. If a landlord texts "Rent is $1,200/month starting June 1" and the tenant texts "Agreed" and pays $1,200, a court could find that a lease exists. Written leases are still far preferable.
