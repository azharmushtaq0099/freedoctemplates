How to Dissolve an LLC: Step-by-Step Guide (2026)

Not closing an LLC properly is one of the most common and expensive mistakes small business owners make — the LLC keeps accruing annual fees, taxes, and potential liability even after you stop operating.

Step 1: Vote to Dissolve

Check your LLC operating agreement — most require a majority vote (or supermajority) of members to approve dissolution. Document the vote in writing: a "resolution to dissolve" signed by the requisite number of members. For single-member LLCs, the sole member's written decision is sufficient.

Once the vote is documented, set an effective dissolution date and stop taking on new business obligations.

Step 2: Wind Up Business Affairs

Before filing dissolution with the state, you must "wind up" the LLC: (1) Complete all pending contracts or formally terminate them. (2) Collect all outstanding accounts receivable. (3) Pay all outstanding debts and obligations — including employee wages, vendor bills, taxes. (4) Notify creditors of the dissolution (some states require a formal creditor notice period of 90–180 days). (5) Close business bank accounts and credit cards after all checks clear.

Priority of payments: creditors are paid before members receive any distributions. Paying member distributions before paying creditors can expose members to personal liability for the shortfall.

Step 3: File Articles of Dissolution with Your State

Every state requires a formal dissolution filing with the Secretary of State (or equivalent agency). The document is typically called "Articles of Dissolution," "Certificate of Termination," or "Statement of Dissolution." Filing fees range from $0 (Iowa) to $200 (California).

Required information: LLC name and state ID number, effective date of dissolution, confirmation that debts have been paid, and member/manager signatures. Some states also require a tax clearance certificate from the state revenue department before accepting the dissolution filing.

Step 4: Handle Final Tax Filings

File a final federal tax return for the LLC (check "final return" on the form). If you have employees, file all final payroll tax returns and W-2s. Close the business's EIN by sending a letter to the IRS. If your LLC was registered in multiple states, file dissolution in every state where you were registered.

Keep business records for at least 7 years after dissolution — IRS audit windows and state audit periods can extend that long. Records include the articles of dissolution, the final tax returns, member voting records, and all financial statements.

FAQ

What happens if I just stop operating my LLC without dissolving it?
The LLC continues to exist legally. You continue to owe annual report fees, franchise taxes, and registered agent fees. The state can administratively dissolve the LLC for non-payment — but even then, you may still be liable for taxes owed during the period before dissolution. Failing to dissolve properly also keeps the LLC vulnerable to lawsuits related to past activities.
Can I dissolve an LLC with outstanding debt?
You can file for dissolution while debts exist, but the winding-up process requires paying known creditors before distributing assets to members. If the LLC is insolvent (debts exceed assets), consult a business attorney before dissolving — members and managers who distribute assets to themselves before paying creditors can face personal liability for the shortfall.
How long does LLC dissolution take?
Processing time varies significantly by state: California 1–2 weeks, Delaware 24 hours (expedited), Texas 2–4 weeks, New York 3–6 weeks, Florida 5–7 business days. Expedited filing options are available in most states for a higher fee. After the state approves dissolution, the LLC is terminated.
Do I need a lawyer to dissolve an LLC?
For a simple LLC with one or two members, few assets, and no outstanding disputes, self-filing dissolution is generally straightforward. For LLCs with multiple members who disagree, significant assets to distribute, employees, ongoing contracts, or substantial debt, consulting a business attorney is strongly recommended to avoid personal liability exposure.
