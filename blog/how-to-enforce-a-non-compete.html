How to Enforce a Non-Compete Agreement (Employer's Guide)

When a former employee joins a competitor in violation of a non-compete, you have a narrow window to act. Courts grant injunctions quickly — but only if you move fast and have the right facts.

Step 1: Assess Enforceability First

Before spending money on litigation, evaluate whether your non-compete is likely enforceable in your state. If the employee is in California, Minnesota, North Dakota, or Oklahoma, stop — courts there will not enforce employee non-competes regardless of what the contract says (and in California, trying to enforce one can expose you to statutory penalties under AB 2288).

In states that enforce non-competes, the threshold questions: Is the duration reasonable (generally 1–2 years max)? Is the geographic scope appropriate for the actual business territory? Is the activity restriction limited to truly competitive work, not just "anything in the industry"? Did the employee receive adequate consideration when they signed? If the answers are yes, you have a viable case.

Step 2: Send a Cease and Desist Letter

Before filing suit, send a detailed cease and desist letter to both the former employee and the new employer. The letter to the new employer is important — it puts them on legal notice of the non-compete, which can make them liable for tortious interference if they continue employing the person knowing they are in breach.

Some new employers, upon receiving a cease and desist, immediately reassign the employee or require them to resolve the dispute before continuing employment. This is the fastest resolution and costs the least. Give a 10–14 day response deadline.

Step 3: File for a Temporary Restraining Order (TRO)

If the C&D is ignored and the competitive activity continues, file for a TRO in state court. A TRO can be obtained within 24–72 hours of filing, ex parte (without the defendant present), if you can show: (1) likelihood of success on the merits, (2) irreparable harm (harm that money damages can't fix — like loss of trade secrets or client relationships), (3) balance of hardships favors the employer, and (4) the injunction serves the public interest.

"Irreparable harm" is the hardest element. Courts are skeptical that losing one employee to a competitor is "irreparable." You need evidence of specific harm — trade secret disclosure, solicitation of specific named clients, using confidential information.

What Courts Award and What They Don't

If you win the TRO and then the preliminary injunction hearing (usually 10–14 days later), the court orders the employee to stop the competitive activity for the restriction period. They may also order them to resign from the new employer.

Damages for non-compete violations are notoriously hard to prove — courts require concrete financial harm traceable to the breach. Most non-compete cases seek injunctive relief only, not damages. If trade secrets were misappropriated, the federal Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA) provides a separate cause of action with potentially stronger remedies.

FAQ

Does a non-compete survive termination for any reason?
Yes, in most cases. Non-competes typically state they survive termination "for any reason." However, some courts are more skeptical of enforcing a non-compete against an employee who was laid off (not fired for cause, not quit) — especially if the non-compete provides no compensation during the restriction period. Massachusetts, for example, requires paying at least 50% of base salary during the restriction period.
Can you sue the new employer for hiring someone covered by a non-compete?
Yes — this is called tortious interference with contract. Once you send the new employer a C&D letter, they have actual notice of the non-compete. Continuing to employ the person after notice can make them jointly liable. The lawsuit would allege tortious interference with your contractual relationship with the former employee.
How quickly does a TRO proceeding move?
Very quickly by litigation standards. A TRO application can be filed and heard within 1–3 days of filing. A preliminary injunction hearing follows in 10–14 days. This is why acting immediately (within days of learning of the breach) is critical — courts are less likely to grant a TRO if you waited weeks before acting.
What if the non-compete was never signed?
If no signed non-compete agreement exists, you generally cannot enforce one. Verbal non-compete agreements are generally unenforceable (they fall under the statute of frauds in most states). The exception: if trade secrets were misappropriated, you can still pursue a DTSA claim even without a non-compete agreement.
