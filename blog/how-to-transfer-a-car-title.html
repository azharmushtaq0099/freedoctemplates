How to Transfer a Car Title After a Private Sale

The title is what makes you the legal owner. A bill of sale proves you bought the car; the title transfer makes it official in state records.

Seller's Steps Before the Sale

Before handing over the keys: (1) Locate the title. If it's lost, apply for a duplicate through your state DMV before selling — it can take 1–4 weeks. (2) Satisfy any liens. If there is a lienholder (bank, credit union) on the title, they must release the lien before you can transfer clear title. Contact the lienholder and pay off the loan or get a lien release. (3) Complete the seller's section on the back of the title: odometer reading, sale price, date, buyer's name and address, and your signature. Some states require notarization.

Write all information exactly as required by your state — errors on the title require a correction process at the DMV.

The Bill of Sale

A bill of sale is separate from the title but equally important. It records: VIN, year/make/model, sale price, date, and both parties' signatures. Some states require a bill of sale for title transfer; others just need the title. Even where not required, the bill of sale protects both parties: the buyer has proof of purchase price (for tax purposes), and the seller has documentation of the transaction.

Retain a signed copy. The IRS and state DMV may ask about the sale price.

Buyer's Steps After the Sale

After taking possession of the signed title and bill of sale: (1) Take both documents to your county DMV or motor vehicle office within the deadline (typically 30 days). (2) Bring government-issued photo ID. (3) Pay the title transfer fee ($15–$85 depending on state) and sales tax on the purchase price. (4) If the vehicle has an odometer over 10 years old, the odometer disclosure requirement may not apply (varies by state).

In most states, you must have proof of insurance before driving the vehicle off the lot or registering it. Do not drive an unregistered, uninsured vehicle to the DMV.

Seller's Steps After the Sale

(1) Remove license plates — in most states, plates belong to the seller, not the vehicle. Transfer them to your new vehicle or turn them in. (2) File a "Notice of Transfer" or "Release of Liability" with your state DMV (online in most states — free). This officially removes you from responsibility for the vehicle. Without this notice, you can receive red-light camera tickets, toll violations, and liability claims from the new owner's accidents until the buyer completes the title transfer. (3) Cancel or transfer your auto insurance policy.

FAQ

What if the title is in both names with "or" vs "and"?
"Owner A or Owner B" — either person can sign the title transfer alone. "Owner A and Owner B" — both must sign. This is why "and" joint ownership creates complications when one owner is unavailable (divorce, death, estrangement). Check the exact language on your title before the sale.
What if the buyer never registers the car?
This is why the seller's "Notice of Transfer" is critical. If you file it promptly, you are released from liability for anything the vehicle does after the sale. If you don't file it and the buyer never registers the car, you can receive violations, property tax bills, and even be implicated in accidents associated with the vehicle.
Can I sell a car without a title?
Technically yes in some states for very old vehicles, but it is strongly discouraged. Without a title, the buyer cannot register the vehicle or prove legal ownership. The car may have an undisclosed lien, may be stolen, or may be a salvage vehicle. Never buy a car without a clean title.
What is a "title jumping" violation?
"Title jumping" occurs when a buyer resells a vehicle without transferring the title into their name first — the title skips a link in the chain. This is illegal in all states and can result in fines and difficulty establishing clean title. Dealers are specifically prohibited from title jumping.
