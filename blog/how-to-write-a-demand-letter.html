How to Write a Demand Letter: Template, Format & Tips

A well-written demand letter can resolve a dispute without going to court — and if you do need to sue, it shows the judge you tried to resolve it first.

What a Demand Letter Is For

A demand letter formally notifies someone that they owe you money, have violated a contract, caused you harm, or need to take a specific action — and gives them a deadline to respond before you take legal action. Courts in most states require evidence that you attempted to resolve a dispute before filing, and a demand letter satisfies this requirement.

Common uses: unpaid invoices, security deposit disputes, personal injury claims, breach of contract (contractor didn't finish work, business partner didn't pay), property damage, and landlord-tenant disputes.

Required Elements

Every demand letter should include: (1) Your full name and contact information at the top. (2) The recipient's full name and address. (3) The date. (4) A clear statement of what happened — the facts, in chronological order, without editorializing. (5) The specific legal basis if applicable ("This constitutes breach of contract under our agreement dated March 1, 2026"). (6) The exact amount or action demanded. (7) A specific deadline — typically 14–30 days. (8) A statement of next steps if demands are not met: "If I do not receive payment by [date], I will file a claim in [court] without further notice."

Sign the letter and keep a copy. Send via certified mail return receipt requested, and also email if you have the address — multiple delivery methods create a stronger paper trail.

Tone and Language

Effective demand letters are: factual, specific, and free of emotional language. "You stole from me and you're a liar" is less effective than "As documented in invoices 101–103, a total of $4,750 remains outstanding past the 30-day payment term in our agreement."

Avoid: threats of criminal prosecution for civil matters (this can constitute extortion in some states), vague threats ("take all legal action available"), or negotiating against yourself by starting at a lower number than you're owed. Demand the full amount you are owed.

Small Claims vs. Demand Letter

For disputes under $5,000–$25,000 (limits vary by state), small claims court is designed for self-represented parties. The demand letter is still important — many small claims courts require proof that you demanded payment before filing, and some defendants will pay after receiving the letter rather than face court.

For amounts over the small claims limit, you will need a lawyer or represent yourself in regular civil court. The demand letter establishes the timeline, documents the amount, and demonstrates good faith — all of which matter to a judge.

FAQ

Does a demand letter need to be from a lawyer?
No. Anyone can write and send a demand letter. Letters on attorney letterhead are often taken more seriously by businesses and insurance companies, but a well-documented letter from an individual is fully effective. For amounts under $5,000, writing it yourself is almost always cost-effective.
What if the recipient ignores my demand letter?
File in small claims court (for amounts under your state's limit) or hire an attorney for larger claims. The demand letter becomes evidence that you notified the defendant of the claim and gave them an opportunity to resolve it before litigation — judges appreciate this.
Should I send the demand letter by email or certified mail?
Both if possible. Certified mail with return receipt creates a legal record that the letter was delivered and signed for. Email creates a timestamp showing delivery. If the recipient claims they never received the letter, you have two forms of delivery evidence.
Can a demand letter be used against me?
Your letter can be read in court, so be accurate. Do not overstate your damages, claim injuries that didn't happen, or threaten actions you won't take. Courts can use exaggerated demand letters to undermine your credibility.
