Independent Contractor Agreement: 8 Clauses You Must Have

An independent contractor agreement does two jobs: it documents the working relationship (protecting against misclassification claims) and defines the project terms (preventing scope disputes).

1. Contractor Status and Control Clause

This is the most legally important clause. It must state that: (1) The contractor is an independent contractor, not an employee. (2) The contractor controls the means and methods of their work, not the company. (3) The contractor provides their own tools and equipment (or specify if company provides tools, and why). (4) The contractor may work for other clients.

Courts look past labels to the actual working relationship. The clause alone doesn't create contractor status — but it documents the intent, and when paired with a working relationship that actually reflects contractor independence, it is strong evidence.

2. Scope of Work and Deliverables

Define exactly what the contractor will produce or perform: specific deliverables, acceptance criteria, revision rounds, and delivery timeline. Vague scope is the source of almost every contractor dispute.

"Develop a mobile app" is a trap. "Develop an iOS app as specified in Exhibit A (attached) including features X, Y, Z, delivered as working source code by March 31, 2026, with up to 3 rounds of revision based on client feedback" is a contract.

3. Payment, Invoicing, and Late Payment

Specify: rate (hourly or fixed), total amount (if fixed), payment schedule (milestone-based, monthly, or on delivery), invoicing terms (contractor invoices within X days of milestone, client pays within Y days of receipt), and a late payment penalty (typically 1.5%/month).

For fixed-price contracts: define a change order process. If the client requests work outside the defined scope, the contractor submits a change order with an additional price, and work proceeds only after written approval. Without this, scope creep is unpaid labor.

4–8. IP Assignment, Confidentiality, Termination, Governing Law, No-Hire

IP Assignment: "Upon receipt of full payment, Contractor assigns all intellectual property rights in the Work Product to Company." Add a provision that the contractor retains rights to pre-existing work (tools, libraries, prior code) they bring to the project. Confidentiality: define what is confidential and how long the obligation lasts (2–5 years is standard). Termination: either party may terminate with 14 days' notice; client pays for work completed to date. Governing law: name the state. No-hire/non-solicitation: company won't hire contractor's employees; contractor won't solicit company's clients (for 12–18 months after engagement ends — this is generally more enforceable than a non-compete).

FAQ

Does an IC agreement prevent worker misclassification claims?
It helps significantly but does not guarantee protection. Courts and government agencies look at the actual working relationship, not just the contract label. The agreement documents intent. If the working relationship also reflects contractor independence (contractor sets own hours, uses own tools, works for multiple clients, controls how work is done), the combination is strong evidence of contractor status.
Should the IC agreement or a SOW define the scope?
For ongoing contractor relationships, a master IC agreement covers the general terms (status, IP, confidentiality, payment terms, termination) and individual Statements of Work (SOWs) define each specific project. This is cleaner than updating one long agreement for every new project.
Can the same contractor agreement be used with all contractors?
For similar types of work, yes. But adjust the scope of work, payment terms, and IP provisions for each engagement. An IC agreement for a software developer needs different IP terms than one for a photographer or marketing consultant — the default copyright rules differ for these types of work.
What if the contractor refuses to sign an IP assignment?
Some contractors (especially photographers, illustrators, writers) routinely negotiate to retain copyright and grant only a license. This is legitimate. If the contractor retains copyright, make sure the license terms are extremely clear: what can you use the work for, in what channels, for how long, and can you modify it? An exclusive perpetual license is close to assignment in practical effect.
