Joint Tenancy vs Tenancy in Common: What's the Difference?

How you hold title to real property with another person determines what happens when one owner dies, wants to sell, or gets divorced. Getting it wrong has major consequences.

Joint Tenancy With Right of Survivorship

In a joint tenancy, two or more people own equal shares of a property, and when one owner dies, their share automatically passes to the surviving owner(s) — bypassing probate entirely. The surviving owner receives the deceased owner's share by operation of law, simply by recording a death certificate.

Requirements for creating a joint tenancy: all owners must acquire title at the same time, through the same deed, with equal shares, and with the right of survivorship explicitly stated in the deed. Missing any of these requirements typically creates a tenancy in common instead.

Tenancy in Common

In a tenancy in common, two or more people own a property with defined percentage interests (which can be unequal), and each owner's share passes through their estate when they die — it goes to whoever they name in their will (or to heirs under intestate succession if there is no will), not automatically to the other owners.

A tenancy in common owner can sell or mortgage their individual share without the other owners' consent (though a buyer of a partial interest rarely wants to own property with strangers). This is the default form of co-ownership when the deed does not specify.

Which Is Better for Married Couples?

Many married couples hold property as joint tenants so that on the first spouse's death, the surviving spouse automatically owns the entire property without probate. Simple and fast.

However, in community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin), married couples often hold property as "community property with right of survivorship" — which gives both the automatic survivorship of joint tenancy and the tax advantage of community property (full step-up in cost basis on the first death, saving capital gains tax when the survivor sells).

Severing a Joint Tenancy

A joint tenancy can be "severed" (converted to a tenancy in common) by one owner transferring their share — even to themselves via a deed to a trustee, in some states. Once severed, the survivorship feature disappears. This is sometimes used in estate planning to ensure a share goes to children from a prior marriage rather than automatically to a spouse.

A tenancy in common can be converted to joint tenancy only if all owners agree and execute a new deed explicitly creating joint tenancy with right of survivorship.

FAQ

Can joint tenancy owners be forced to sell?
A joint tenant who wants to sell but cannot get the other tenant's agreement can file a "partition action" in court. The court can either order the property divided (partition in kind) or order it sold with proceeds split proportionally (partition by sale). Partition actions are costly and take 1–2 years — an exit strategy clause in a co-ownership agreement is far better.
What happens to a joint tenancy in a divorce?
In most states, divorce automatically severs a joint tenancy and converts it to a tenancy in common — preventing the "accidental inheritance" of the property by an estranged spouse. The divorce settlement agreement or court order then determines what happens to the tenants-in-common shares.
Can a tenant in common leave their share to whoever they want?
Yes. Unlike a joint tenant (whose share passes to the surviving co-owner automatically), a tenant in common can leave their share to anyone via their will. If they die without a will, the share passes to heirs under intestate succession law — not necessarily the other owners.
Should unmarried co-buyers use joint tenancy or tenancy in common?
Unmarried co-buyers (partners, friends, siblings) usually benefit from tenancy in common with a co-ownership agreement that specifies: what percentage each person owns, how expenses are split, what happens if one wants to sell or refinance, and a right of first refusal (the other owner gets to buy out the selling owner before they sell to a stranger).
