What Is Just Cause Eviction? Tenant Protections Explained

In most of the US, landlords can end a month-to-month tenancy for any reason — or no reason — with 30 days' notice. Just cause eviction laws change that entirely.

What "Just Cause" Means

A just cause eviction ordinance prohibits landlords from evicting tenants without a legally recognized reason. The permitted reasons — called "just causes" — typically fall into two categories: at-fault causes (non-payment of rent, lease violations, criminal activity, subletting without permission) and no-fault causes (owner move-in, substantial renovation requiring vacancy, property demolition, Ellis Act withdrawal).

Without a just cause ordinance, landlords can issue a "no-fault, no-cause" termination — ending the tenancy without stating any reason. With just cause protection, that is illegal.

Where Just Cause Laws Apply

Statewide: California (AB 1482, applies to most buildings over 15 years old), Oregon (SB 608), Washington (HB 1236). These are the only states with statewide just cause laws.

Major cities with local just cause ordinances: New York City (Good Cause Eviction Law), San Francisco, Los Angeles, Seattle, Portland, Oakland, Berkeley, San Jose, Minneapolis, Chicago (limited), Washington DC, Newark NJ, and dozens of others. If you live in a major metro, check your city's tenant rights office.

No-Fault Just Cause: Owner Move-In and Ellis Act

Most just cause laws allow a no-fault eviction if the owner genuinely intends to move in (owner move-in, or OMI). Requirements are strict: the owner must actually move in within 90 days, stay for a minimum period (12 months in SF), and pay the tenant substantial relocation assistance (often 1–3 months' rent).

The Ellis Act (California) allows landlords to evict all tenants from a building permanently if they are "going out of the rental business." San Francisco Ellis Act evictions require 120 days' notice (1 year for elderly/disabled tenants) and relocation payments.

Proving Just Cause in Court

Landlords in just cause jurisdictions must state the reason for termination in the notice itself — a blank "notice to vacate" is invalid. If a tenant fights the eviction, the landlord must prove the stated cause. Filing an eviction without valid just cause can expose the landlord to hefty penalties: wrongful eviction damages, attorney's fees, and sometimes three times actual damages.

For tenants: if you receive a no-fault notice in a just cause jurisdiction, research whether your building and tenancy are covered before moving out. Just cause exemptions are common (new construction under 15 years, single-family homes in some states, owner-occupied buildings with 2–4 units).

FAQ

Does just cause eviction apply to month-to-month tenancies?
Yes — that is the whole point. In most states, landlords can end month-to-month tenancies at will with 30 days' notice. Just cause laws eliminate this ability, requiring a stated and legally recognized reason for all terminations, including month-to-month.
What happens if a landlord evicts a tenant without just cause in a covered city?
The tenant can sue for wrongful eviction. Remedies vary by jurisdiction: San Francisco awards the greater of actual damages or three times the monthly rent, plus attorney's fees. New York City's Good Cause Eviction Law allows tenants to raise just cause as a defense in eviction court, which can result in dismissal.
Are all tenants covered by just cause laws?
No. Most just cause laws have significant exemptions: newer construction (buildings under 10–15 years old), single-family homes (where the landlord has disclosed the exemption), condos being sold to owner-occupants, vacation rentals, and owner-occupied buildings with 2–4 units. Always verify whether your specific unit is covered.
Can a tenant waive just cause protections?
No. Tenant protections created by law cannot be waived by lease agreement. A lease clause that says "tenant waives just cause protections" is void and unenforceable. Landlords cannot contract around local tenant protection ordinances.
