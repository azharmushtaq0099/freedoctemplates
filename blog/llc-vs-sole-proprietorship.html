LLC vs Sole Proprietorship: Which Is Better for Your Business?

The most important question isn't tax treatment — it's personal liability. If your business gets sued or can't pay its debts, do you want to lose your house?

Personal Liability: The Core Difference

A sole proprietorship is the simplest business structure — you and your business are legally the same entity. Business debts are your personal debts. If your business is sued and loses, the plaintiff can go after your personal bank accounts, car, and home.

An LLC (Limited Liability Company) creates a legal wall between you and the business. If the LLC is sued or can't pay its debts, creditors can only go after LLC assets — not your personal assets. This is the primary reason to form an LLC. The protection is not absolute (courts can "pierce the corporate veil" if you comingle personal and business funds or treat the LLC as your alter ego), but it is significant.

Tax Treatment

Both sole proprietorships and single-member LLCs are taxed as "pass-through" entities by default — business income flows to your personal tax return (Schedule C) and you pay self-employment tax (15.3% on net profit up to the Social Security wage base in 2026).

The difference: an LLC can elect S-Corp tax treatment, allowing you to split income between a salary and distributions. Distributions are not subject to self-employment tax. This can save $5,000–$20,000+ per year for profitable businesses. This election makes sense roughly when net profit consistently exceeds $50,000–$60,000/year.

Cost and Complexity

A sole proprietorship has zero formation cost and zero ongoing compliance requirements. You just start operating. There's nothing to file, no annual reports, and no fees (unless you file a DBA).

An LLC requires: state filing fee ($50–$500 depending on state), registered agent (you or a service), operating agreement, separate bank account, and in many states an annual report and fee. California adds an $800/year minimum franchise tax. Delaware LLCs pay $300/year. New York adds a publication requirement that can cost $1,000+.

When to Use Each

Sole proprietorship makes sense for: very low-risk service businesses (consulting, freelancing with low liability exposure), testing a business idea before committing, or businesses with very low annual net income where LLC costs aren't worth it.

LLC makes sense for: any business that could be sued (services where you give advice, physical goods, client-facing work), businesses with real assets (equipment, inventory, IP), any business with employees, businesses where personal liability protection justifies the cost, and any business earning consistently over $50K/year (where S-Corp election can save on taxes).

FAQ

Can I convert from a sole proprietorship to an LLC?
Yes. File LLC articles of organization with your state, get a new EIN from the IRS, open a new business bank account, and transfer contracts and assets to the LLC. Notify clients, vendors, and your bank. The conversion is prospective — you cannot retroactively protect yourself from past sole proprietorship debts.
Does an LLC protect me from my own negligence?
An LLC protects you from business debts and lawsuits arising from the business's operations or the negligence of employees. It does NOT protect you from your own personal negligence — if you personally cause harm (run a red light in the company van, give bad advice to a client), you can still be personally liable. Professional liability insurance covers this gap.
Do I need a business license for either?
Business structure (LLC vs sole proprietorship) is separate from licensing. Most businesses need some combination of local business license, professional license, seller's permit, or industry-specific permits regardless of structure. Check your city, county, and state requirements.
Which is better for a side hustle?
For a very small side hustle with low liability risk (selling on Etsy, writing, design), sole proprietorship is fine. Once income exceeds $10,000/year or the nature of the work creates liability exposure (physical products, advice, services where errors harm clients), the LLC's protection is worth the cost.
