How to Get Power of Attorney for an Elderly Parent

The most important thing to know: a power of attorney can only be granted by someone who is mentally competent. If you wait until your parent has dementia, it may be too late.

Why Timing Is Everything

A power of attorney requires the principal to have legal capacity — they must understand what they are signing, what authority they are giving, and to whom. Early-stage dementia often does not eliminate capacity; it is a spectrum. But as dementia progresses, there comes a point where a court would not recognize any POA signed after that threshold.

The family conversation about POA is uncomfortable. It feels like planning for decline. But the cost of not having a POA — court-ordered conservatorship — is far higher: $5,000–$30,000+ in legal fees, months of delay, and a judge making decisions instead of your family.

Which Type of POA Does an Elderly Parent Need?

For most aging parents, you need two documents: (1) A Durable Financial POA — authorizes a child or trusted person to manage bank accounts, pay bills, file taxes, manage investments, and handle real estate. "Durable" means it remains effective if the parent becomes incapacitated. (2) A Healthcare (Medical) POA — authorizes someone to make medical decisions, choose providers, access medical records (HIPAA), and make end-of-life decisions if the parent cannot.

These should be accompanied by a living will (advance directive) stating the parent's own wishes about life support, CPR, and feeding tubes — so the healthcare agent knows what the parent would want.

How to Prepare and Execute the Documents

Most states provide forms or templates for both a financial and healthcare POA. Steps: (1) Prepare the documents with your parent — do not prepare them without their involvement and genuine consent. (2) Have an attorney review if there are complex assets or family disputes. (3) Sign before a notary public. (4) Most states also require 1–2 witnesses (who cannot be the agent, their spouse, or a beneficiary of the parent's estate). (5) Give copies to the agent, successor agents, the parent's primary care physician, and all financial institutions involved.

For healthcare POA specifically: deliver a copy to the parent's primary care doctor, any specialist, and the hospital system where they typically receive care. Register it if your state has a healthcare directive registry.

What If Your Parent Can't Sign a POA?

If a parent has already lost mental capacity, no POA can be signed. The family's only option is a court-ordered guardianship or conservatorship. In guardianship, the court appoints someone to make personal and medical decisions. In conservatorship, the court appoints someone to manage finances.

This process takes 2–6 months, costs $5,000–$30,000+ in legal fees, requires an attorney in most states, involves a court investigator visiting the parent, and results in annual court reporting requirements. The conservator must account to the court for every financial decision. It is avoidable with timely POA planning.

FAQ

Can a parent with early dementia still sign a POA?
Possibly — it depends on whether they have "testamentary capacity" at the time of signing. Courts look at: does the person understand what a POA is, who they are appointing, and what authority they are giving? Early dementia may not eliminate this capacity. A physician's letter documenting capacity at the time of signing (even with a diagnosis) greatly strengthens the POA's validity.
Can siblings challenge a POA?
Yes. A sibling who believes the parent lacked capacity, was unduly influenced, or was defrauded when signing the POA can file a legal challenge. This is why having the POA signed with witnesses, in front of a notary, and with a physician capacity letter is so important. The more documentation of knowing, voluntary consent, the harder the challenge.
What authority does a financial POA give my child over my accounts?
A durable financial POA typically authorizes the agent to: access and manage all bank accounts, pay bills, file tax returns, buy and sell real estate, manage investments, apply for benefits, and operate a business. You can limit the scope — for example, authorizing bill pay only, not real estate transactions. Be specific about any limitations.
Is a POA from another state valid?
Generally yes. Most states have laws recognizing POAs executed in other states if valid under that state's law at the time of signing. However, some financial institutions are skeptical of out-of-state POAs and may require their own forms. Always call ahead before trying to use an out-of-state POA for a major transaction.
