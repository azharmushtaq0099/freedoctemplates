What Is a Cease and Desist Letter? How to Write One That Works

A cease and desist letter is not a court order — it is a formal demand. Its power lies in credibility: it signals you are serious, documents the violation, and creates a record before litigation.

When to Use a Cease and Desist Letter

Common situations: (1) Copyright infringement — someone is using your photos, writing, or creative work without permission. (2) Trademark infringement — a business is using your brand name, logo, or a confusingly similar one. (3) Defamation — false statements of fact are being made about you or your business. (4) Harassment or stalking. (5) Breach of contract — a former employee is violating a non-compete or NDA.

A cease and desist letter is most effective when the recipient has something to lose by continuing the behavior — business reputation, a professional license, or facing imminent litigation. It is less effective against anonymous online harassers or judgment-proof defendants.

What a Cease and Desist Letter Must Include

An effective cease and desist letter includes: (1) Your identity and the specific legal right being violated. (2) A precise description of the violation — dates, locations, URLs, specific acts. (3) The specific demand — stop the activity by [date], remove the infringing content, destroy infringing copies, etc. (4) A response deadline — typically 10–14 business days. (5) The legal consequences if demands are not met — lawsuit, injunction, damages. (6) A statement that the letter is being kept as evidence.

Tone should be firm and factual, not emotional or threatening beyond legitimate legal consequences. Threatening criminal prosecution for a civil matter, or making demands you have no legal basis for, can backfire — and in some states, an abusive demand letter can itself be grounds for a malicious prosecution counterclaim.

Does It Have to Come from a Lawyer?

No. Anyone can send a cease and desist letter. A letter on attorney letterhead carries more psychological weight — recipients tend to take it more seriously. But a well-written letter from an individual can be just as legally significant: it documents notice, establishes the timeline, and forces the recipient to respond.

For high-stakes situations (significant infringement, business-threatening defamation, large contract breaches), an attorney-drafted letter is worth the investment. For smaller matters, a well-documented self-written letter is a reasonable first step.

What Happens After You Send It

Three possible responses: (1) The recipient complies — they stop the behavior, respond with a settlement offer, or simply go quiet. This is the desired outcome and happens more often than people expect. (2) The recipient disputes your claim in writing — their response may reveal weaknesses in your position and open a dialogue. (3) The recipient ignores it or escalates — in this case, your letter becomes exhibit A in your subsequent lawsuit.

Keep a copy of the letter, the date sent, proof of delivery (certified mail or email read receipt), and any response. If you need to go to court, this documentation is critical.

FAQ

Is a cease and desist letter legally binding?
No. A cease and desist letter is a demand, not a court order. The recipient is not legally required to comply. However, ignoring it and continuing the behavior can be used as evidence of willful infringement or bad faith — which increases damages in subsequent litigation. For a legally binding stop order, you must obtain a court-issued injunction.
Can sending a cease and desist letter backfire?
Yes. If your legal claim is weak or unsupported, the recipient may countersue for wrongful use of legal process. Public figures sometimes "Streisand Effect" a cease and desist — sending it generates publicity that draws far more attention to the issue than the original violation. Evaluate whether sending one serves your actual goals before proceeding.
How long does a cease and desist letter take to work?
If it works, typically within the 10–14 day response window. Some recipients comply immediately; others negotiate. If the recipient is a business worried about its reputation or legal costs, response can come within days.
Can I send a cease and desist for online defamation?
Yes, and it's often the first step. Include: the exact false statements, where they were published (URLs, dates), and why they are false and defamatory. Request removal and retraction. If the content is on a platform, also file a platform takedown request simultaneously — defamation reports on Google, Yelp, Facebook, etc. are evaluated separately from your letter to the poster.
