What to Include in a Freelancer Contract (2026 Checklist)

Handshake deals in freelancing lead to scope creep, late payments, and ownership disputes. A one-page contract prevents all three.

Scope of Work: The Most Important Clause

The scope of work defines exactly what the freelancer will deliver and what they will not. This is the single most important clause because it defines "done" — the line between satisfactory completion and scope creep.

Be specific: not "a website" but "a 5-page responsive website including homepage, about, services, contact, and blog index page, built in WordPress with the provided theme, with 3 rounds of revisions." Specify what is excluded ("does not include SEO optimization, hosting setup, or stock photo licensing"). Every word you add to scope reduces future disputes.

Payment Terms

Specify: rate (hourly or project-based), total amount, payment schedule (50% upfront, 50% on delivery is standard for new client relationships; milestones for longer projects), payment method (PayPal, bank transfer, check), invoice timing, and late payment penalty (typically 1.5% per month on overdue balances).

For ongoing retainer relationships: define the monthly retainer amount, what it covers (number of hours or deliverables), what is billed additionally, and the notice period to end the retainer.

Intellectual Property: Who Owns the Work?

By default under US copyright law, an independent contractor retains ownership of the work they create — not the client. This means if you hire a freelancer without a written IP assignment, you may not legally own the website, logo, or code they built for you.

Your contract should include an IP assignment clause: "Upon receipt of full payment, Contractor assigns all intellectual property rights in the Work to Client." Alternatively, grant the client a license to use the work rather than full ownership (common for photographers and illustrators who retain copyright but license usage).

Termination, Revisions, and Disputes

Termination: either party should be able to terminate with 14–30 days' written notice. Include a kill fee clause — if the client cancels after work has begun, they pay a percentage of the remaining contract (typically 25–50%) to compensate the freelancer for the lost opportunity.

Revisions: specify how many revision rounds are included. "Unlimited revisions" is a trap — set a number (2–3) and define what constitutes a revision vs a new request. Disputes: include a governing state law clause and whether disputes go to arbitration or court. For small contracts, specifying the client's local small claims court is efficient.

FAQ

Should a freelancer or the client draft the contract?
Whoever sends the contract sets the baseline terms. Freelancers who send their own contract control the IP assignment language, revision limits, and payment terms. Clients who send the contract often include clauses requiring full IP assignment, unlimited revisions, and no kill fee. Having your own template ready and sending it first gives you negotiating leverage.
Is a verbal freelance agreement enforceable?
Verbal contracts are generally enforceable for services, but proving what was agreed is extremely difficult. Courts in most states require written evidence for contracts over $500 (under the statute of frauds). More practically: if the scope, price, and IP ownership weren't written down, both parties will remember them differently the moment a dispute arises.
Can a client sue a freelancer for missing a deadline?
Yes, if the deadline was a specified contract term. A freelancer who materially breaches by missing deadlines can be liable for the client's damages (costs of hiring someone else to finish, lost revenue if the delay caused measurable harm). Force majeure clauses (covering unavoidable delays) are worth including.
What is a "work made for hire" clause?
Under copyright law, "work made for hire" applies automatically to employees — the employer owns the work. For independent contractors, it only applies to certain specific categories of work (contributions to collective works, motion pictures, translations, instructional texts, etc.) if there is a written agreement. For most freelance work, you need an explicit IP assignment clause — "work made for hire" alone doesn't do the job.
